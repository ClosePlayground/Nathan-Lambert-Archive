Deputy Speaker, I normally raise fairly specific Preston Reservoir matters, but we did have
a debate earlier this week about data centres and AI, and I wanted to pick up some of the
points from that debate. I won't repeat the important points that were made about planning,
energy use and water use, but rather I'd like to pick up on this important notion
that data centres are not really data centres. As we spoke about, these are centres that
are not full of traditional file servers, they are not storing people's data or photos
or anything else. Rather, they are predominantly filled with processing units, GPUs, and what
they actually are are the giant artificial brains that we are building to do our thinking
for us when we use applications like Clawd and ChatGPT. They should properly, Deputy
Speaker, be called compute centres or inference centres, and I do think, I don't think
it's deliberate, but I think calling them data centres makes it sound more innocuous
and underplays just how radical this technology is.
A second thing, Deputy Speaker, that I think causes people to underestimate how radical
this technology is, is the fact that the standard models of Clawd or ChatGPT that
we have on our phones, whilst remarkable technology, are very neutered compared to
the extraordinary capacity of the frontier models that these companies have built already,
let alone the ones they are attempting to build. We have this strange situation
with AI, the reverse of what normally happens. What normally happens is that the people,
the public outside an industry are worried, the people inside an industry, be it fracking
or the nuclear industry, are normally less worried. That's the normal course of things.
With AI, it is the other way round. Even the criticisms of the Greens and the
Victorian socialists are mainly focusing on things on water and energy use. They
are important issues, but when you listen to some of the software engineers and researchers
who have built these frontier models and used them day to day, they are seriously
worried about what it will look like when we deploy at massive scale these artificial
creatures that can outthink humans across a broad domain of tasks. They are worried
not only what that will do economically, but what it might do in a military sense
and what it might do in a very fundamental, existential way for humanity. Of course,
there are all sorts of positive things that can come from this, and I want to be clear
that I am just talking about the long tail risks, but as someone with a little bit of
work experience in this sector, the thing that I am most worried now for my children
is no longer climate change, it's not nuclear weapons, it is where AI might go. I just
encourage the other parties, the Greens and the Liberals, who I think have not
fully yet engaged with this issue, to think clearly about it as the Minister and as our
Labor Government are doing so, because I do think, Deputy Speaker, that this will be
one of the most significant regulatory challenges that we in the Victorian
Government and indeed governments across the globe face in the coming decades.
